What changed on July 31, and what didn't

This is the written version of the video. If you bought something legally in June and are trying to work out whether it is contraband now, start with the two columns below.

Now controlled

  • Delta-8 THC
  • Delta-10 THC
  • THCP and related isomers
  • Vape cartridges, wax, dabs, and concentrates containing them

Concentrates fall in Penalty Group 2. Under one gram is a state jail felony — 180 days to 2 years and a fine up to $10,000.

Not changed

  • Hemp at or below 0.3% delta-9 THC by dry weight
  • Compliant delta-9 gummies and edibles
  • The marijuana possession statute — same law, same weights
  • The Compassionate Use Program for qualifying patients

Two ounces or less of flower is still a Class B misdemeanor, not a felony.

Why nobody warned you

This did not come from the legislature. A 2025 bill that would have banned these products passed and was vetoed. Two special sessions produced no major hemp bill. If you followed the news and concluded Texas had decided not to ban these things, you read it correctly at the time.

What actually happened is that definitions adopted by the Department of State Health Services in 2021 were tied up in litigation for years — which is exactly why these products filled the shelves. The Texas Supreme Court cleared that in May, notices were published in July, and the definitions took effect July 31. Old rules that were finally switched on. There was no signing ceremony and no countdown.

The part almost nobody talks about

The state has to prove what the substance actually is. Not what it looked like, not what the packaging said, not what the officer believed at the roadside. That means laboratory analysis identifying specific compounds and distinguishing Delta-8 from compliant Delta-9, with instrumentation and a chain of custody that holds up.

There is no roadside kit that tells an officer which isomer is in a cartridge. Harris County went through this exact problem in 2019 when the hemp law took effect and the labs could not keep up. That is a real question in every one of these cases, and it is worth asking before anyone starts negotiating.

You do not have to own it to be charged with it

Texas uses constructive possession. If the state can show you knew the item was there and had some control over the space it was in, that can be enough. A cartridge in the cup holder while you drive three friends home, or in a kitchen drawer your roommate uses, can put you in the same position. "It wasn't mine" is rarely a complete defense by itself.

Where these cases are actually won

These cases almost never come from an investigation. They come from traffic stops — a lane change, a tail light, a smell, a consent to search that somebody gave because they did not know they could say no. Which means the case usually turns on why the officer stopped you and what happened in the four minutes after, not on what was in the cartridge.
